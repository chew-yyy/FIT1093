00 / MOTIVATION

Why public-key cryptography exists

Symmetric Key Encryption (SKE) uses the same secret key to encrypt and decrypt — so both sides must first share that key privately. Over an insecure channel, that's the key distribution problem.

The idea (Diffie–Hellman, 1976): make the encryption key public.

  • Encrypt with one key — the public key.
  • Decrypt with a different key — the private key.
  • The public key needn't be secret → no private-key distribution problem.
The core move "Do with the public key, undo with the private key." Because you can publish the do-key without helping an attacker undo, the distribution problem disappears.
01 / PKE vs SKE

How PKE compares, and what it must guarantee

PKESKE
Keys Each user has a public key pk & a private key sk, mathematically related Two users privately share one secret key k
Do / undo Do with pk, undo with sk Do & undo with the same k
Distribution No key-distribution problem Has key-distribution problem
Speed Slower Faster

PKE requirements

  • Functionality (easy for good guys): generate the pk/sk pair, encrypt, decrypt.
  • Security (infeasible for bad guys): get sk from pk; or recover plaintext P from ciphertext C and pk.
02 / WHY MATHS

Why PKE needs number theory (and SKE doesn't)

PKE's two keys must be related in a very particular way: use pk to produce C, then use sk to "cancel out" pk and recover P — yet knowing pk must not let you compute sk. That demands a one-way relationship from sk to pk, which is exactly what number theory provides. SKE has no such requirement, so it needs no such maths.

Two problems, two algorithm families (Week 5) Discrete Logarithm Problem (DLP) → Diffie–Hellman, ElGamal.   Integer Factorisation Problem (IFP) → RSA. Both rest on number-theory problems that are easy one way and infeasible the other.
03 / PRIMES & FACTORS

Primes, factors, and factorisation

  • Prime — exactly two divisors: 1 and itself (2, 3, 5, 7, 11, …).
  • Composite — has a divisor other than 1 and itself (e.g. 4 = 1, 2, 4).
  • 0 and 1 are special — neither prime nor composite.
  • Factor / divisor: x is a factor of n if n ÷ x has remainder 0.
  • Trivial factors = 1 and n itself; non-trivial = anything in between. For 6: trivial {1, 6}, non-trivial {2, 3}. Since 2 & 3 are prime, they're the prime factors.
Unique Prime Factorisation Theorem Every integer factors into a product of prime powers in exactly one way, e.g. 3600 = 2⁴ × 3² × 5². This uniqueness is what makes factoring a meaningful hard problem.
04 / IFP

The Integer Factorisation Problem (IFP)

IFP: given an integer n, compute its prime factorisation. The security of RSA hangs on this being hard.

Easy direction (multiply): primes ──► n // fast, even for huge integers Hard direction (IFP): n ──► primes // infeasibly slow for large n
Analogy · Blend vs un-blend Multiplying two large primes is like blending two paint tins — instant. Recovering the two original primes from the product is like un-blending the paint — the asymmetry is the security.

What "efficient" means

Run-time T depends on the length of the input — how many digits/bits n has (e.g. len₁₀(34)=2, len₂(34)=6).

  • Efficient = T is a polynomial in len(n). Multiplying two 3-digit numbers ≈ 3² = 9 steps.
  • Infeasible = T grows exponentially in len(n) — the case for factoring large n.

Strategy for PKE: design encryption so that attacking it requires factoring, while honest users only ever multiply/exponentiate.

05 / GCD

GCD, Euclid's algorithm, and coprimality

  • GCD(x, y) — the largest integer dividing both. GCD(6, 9) = 3; GCD(48, 64) = 16.
  • Coprime / relatively prime — GCD(x, y) = 1. So 5 & 6 are coprime; 6 & 9 are not (GCD 3).
Surprising fact There's an efficient way to compute GCD — Euclid's algorithm — that needs no factoring at all. This matters: you can test coprimality quickly even when factoring the numbers would be infeasible.
06 / MODULAR ARITHMETIC

Modular arithmetic = clock arithmetic

On a 12-hour clock, after 12 you wrap around: 14 mod 12 = 2, written 14 ≡ 2 (mod 12) — "14 and 2 are congruent modulo 12."

The mod trick — reduce as you go

You can take the mod before combining, which keeps numbers small:

(c + d) mod n ≡ ( (c mod n) + (d mod n) ) mod n // same for − and × Worked answers to the slide questions: 42 + 45 mod 15 = (12 + 0) mod 15 = 12 42 − 88 mod 15 = (12 + 2) mod 15 = 14 // −88 ≡ 2 (mod 15) 42 × 77 mod 15 = (12 × 2) mod 15 = 9
Why this matters Crypto works with huge numbers. Reducing mod n after every step keeps every intermediate value below n — that's what makes modular exponentiation practical on real hardware.
07 / MODULAR INVERSE

Multiplicative inverse mod n

In ordinary arithmetic, the inverse of a is the fraction 1/a. In modular arithmetic, a⁻¹ mod n is an integer b such that a × b ≡ 1 (mod n).

5⁻¹ mod 7 = 3 check: 5 × 3 = 15 ≡ 1 (mod 7) ✓ 2⁻¹ mod 9 = 5 check: 2 × 5 = 10 ≡ 1 (mod 9) ✓ 3⁻¹ mod 11 = 4 check: 3 × 4 = 12 ≡ 1 (mod 11) ✓ // slide Q Division = multiply by the inverse: 2 ÷ 5 mod 7 = 2 × 5⁻¹ mod 7 = 2 × 3 mod 7 = 6
When the inverse does NOT exist a⁻¹ mod n exists only if a and n are coprime (GCD = 1). Example: 4⁻¹ mod 12 doesn't exist because GCD(4, 12) = 4 > 1.
Fact When a and n are coprime, the Extended Euclid algorithm computes a⁻¹ mod n efficiently — again with no factoring. (This is exactly how RSA derives the private key d from e.)
08 / EXPONENTIATION & DLP

Modular exponentiation and the Discrete Log Problem

aᵉ mod n = multiply a by itself e times, mod n. Even when e has hundreds of digits, the square-and-multiply algorithm computes it efficiently — this is the workhorse of both RSA and DH.

Easy direction (exponentiate): a, e ──► b = aᵉ mod n // fast (square & multiply) Hard direction (DLP): b, a, n ──► e // infeasible

Discrete Logarithm Problem (DLP): given b = aᵉ mod n together with a and n, recover the exponent e. Known algorithms (brute force, Number Field Sieve) are exponential/sub-exponential — infeasible for large n.

Two hard problems, side by side IFP: easy to multiply, hard to factor → RSA.   DLP: easy to exponentiate, hard to take the log → Diffie–Hellman, ElGamal.
09 / GENERATING PRIMES

How to find large prime numbers

PKE needs large random primes, generated efficiently. Two questions make that possible:

  • Q1 — can we test primality fast? Trial division is exponential (too slow), but the Miller–Rabin primality test is efficient (no trial division).
  • Q2 — are primes common enough? Yes. The Prime Number Theorem says the fraction of L-bit integers that are prime is ≈ 1 / (0.693 × L). For 1000-bit numbers you expect a prime after ≈ 693 random tries.
Conclusion Keep picking random integers and Miller–Rabin-test them until one passes. Both the test and the hit-rate are efficient, so generating large random primes is practical.
